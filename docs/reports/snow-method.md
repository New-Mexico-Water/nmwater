# Snow by watershed and mountain range: the method

How the snow figures for the site are made, what each one means and where it is weak. The code is in `nmwater/derived/` (`watershed_snow.py`, `snow_ranges.py`, `snow_normal.py`, `snotel_snow.py`) and `nmwater/site/snow.py`; the new-snow estimate is checked against observers in [new-snow-estimate.md](new-snow-estimate.md) (generated by `scripts/new_snow_validation.py`).

## Three different things

| Measure | What it is | Where it comes from | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water in the snow | The depth of water the snowpack would make if melted (snow water equivalent). What feeds rivers and reservoirs. | NOAA SNODAS model, averaged over each place | stock |
| Snow depth | How deep the snow is on the ground. What people ski on. | NOAA SNODAS model, averaged over each place | stock |
| New snow | Snow that fell: the inches added in a day, a week or the winter so far. | Estimated from SNOTEL station readings | flow |

They are correlated but not the same. A foot of fresh powder holds about an inch of water; a foot of old, settled snow holds three or four. Knowing both is how, later, a year in which the snowpack held its water but the rivers did not run as expected can be told apart from one in which it did not.

## SNODAS: water and depth by place

SNODAS is NOAA's daily 1 km snow model, one file per day from 30 September 2003. It combines weather-model output with every satellite, aircraft and station snow observation available. The archive keeps all eight of its products; the snow pages use `swe` and `snow_depth`.

- **Places.** Every HUC8 watershed with at least 1% of its area in New Mexico (80 of them), and ten mountain ranges (below).
- **Mean.** The plain average of the 1 km cells whose centres fall inside the place. A watershed that runs past the grid (Colorado, Texas, Arizona) is averaged over its covered part; the share is recorded.
- **Day.** A SNODAS file is the model's state for its date. Two grids occur (the corner moved by a tenth of a cell in October 2013) and the latitude order changed in September 2026; the masks are built per grid. A file without `swe` or `snow_depth` is skipped and tried again.
- **A watershed is not a mountain range.** A watershed average includes valleys and plains where the snow is thin or absent, so it reads low in a basin like the Rio Grande's; that is why ranges exist as a second view.

## Mountain ranges

Ten ranges: Sangre de Cristo north and south, Jemez, Chama and the Tusas, Sandia and Manzano, Mount Taylor and the Zuni Mountains, Chuska, Sacramento, Gila and Mogollon, San Mateo and Magdalena.

A range is drawn where snow is usual, not where its name is. The rule (all in `catalog/snow_ranges.yaml`):

1. Take the SNODAS water in the snow on six mid-winter days (10, 15 and 20 February; 10, 15 and 20 March) in each of water years 2004 to 2025, and average them cell by cell.
2. Keep cells that average at least **0.8 inch**.
3. Give each kept cell to the nearest range seed (a high point the range is named for), if it is within **80 km**.
4. Clip to the state and drop separate patches smaller than 30 cells.

**Narrow ranges.** The Sandia and Manzano, and the San Mateo and Magdalena, are too narrow for a 1 km model, which smooths them to a fraction of their real snow. They use **0.3 inch** and **15 km**, and the page says their figures read low. They are shown because the Sandia are above the state's largest city and people will look for them; the numbers are a floor, not a measurement.

## Rating against other winters

Each value is compared with the same day in water years **2004 to 2025** (22 winters, the SNODAS record; 1991 to 2020 is not possible for SNODAS).

- **The usual** for a date is the values on that day and three days either side in each of the 22 winters (water years run 1 October to 30 September; 29 February is left out so each has 365 days).
- **The rating** is the mid-rank percentile of today's value among those, in the site's five classes: under the 10th percentile *much below normal*, 10th to 24th *below normal*, 25th to 75th *normal*, 76th to 90th *above normal*, 91st and up *much above normal*.
- At least 20 values are needed to state a usual (15 for new snow, whose stations have fewer winters).
- **Little snow.** When the value and the usual are both under a few hundredths of water (0.05 inch), half an inch of depth, or an inch of new snow, the exporter marks the place `little` and gives it no class: a few hundredths against a few hundredths says nothing. The same marking, and a minimum usual amount (`MIN_USUAL_IN`) below which a watershed is not rated, travel in the bundle so the site and any other user apply them the same way.
- **Percent of usual** is stated only above a larger usual amount (1 inch of water, 5 inches of depth, 10 inches of season new snow) because a percent of almost nothing is noise.

## New snow, from SNOTEL stations

SNOTEL stations (30 in New Mexico) report snow depth and snow water, but not snowfall. The estimate:

> A day counts when the snow depth rises **1 to 30 inches** since the day before **and** the snow-water reading rises **at least 0.1 inch** the same day. The day's new snow is the depth rise.

The water condition is what separates a storm from sensor jitter: counting depth rises alone gave 220 to 440 inches of phantom snow a winter at some stations before the floor was added. A day with no reading, or none the day before, is unknown, never zero.

- **Windows:** the day, the last seven days, and the winter so far (since 1 October). A total needs 85% of its days.
- **Rating:** the mean total over a place's stations, against the same stations' totals on the same dates in each winter. A station counts only with at least 17 winters of record; a year counts only if every counted station reported.
- **Which places have it:** only a watershed or range that contains a station. Others show no new-snow figure.
- **What it is not:** a measurement of snowfall. Snow settles, melts and blows, so a total undercounts what a board would have caught; the check against observers found it at about two-thirds of what they measured, seeing half of their snow days, while the stations sit higher where more falls ([the full comparison](new-snow-estimate.md)).

### What a "day" is

NRCS's daily row dated *D* is the station's reading at the local midnight that **ends** day *D* (it equals the hourly reading at 07:00 UTC on *D*+1 exactly, in every month, because SNOTEL clocks do not change for daylight time). So a rise dated *D* is the snow of the local day *D*, and the newest complete day on the site is **yesterday**. Until the daily row arrives the exporter takes the hourly reading at that same midnight, so the newest day means what the older days mean. A report that says "last 24 hours" would be wrong; the pages say "yesterday".

## What the figures cannot say

- SNODAS is a model. At 1 km it smooths narrow ranges and steep terrain, and in a winter with few station observations it leans on weather-model output. Its water in the snow can differ from a nearby SNOTEL pillow; the site shows both and does not blend them.
- The baseline is 22 winters. "Normal" is normal for 2004 to 2025, not for the 1991 to 2020 period the rest of the site uses for rivers.
- Station-based figures cover only places with a station. Valles Caldera and the Sandia have none within range.
- Provisional data. SNOTEL values for the latest months have not been reviewed.

## Reproduce

```
uv run nmwater watershed-snow          # SNODAS by place (about five minutes the first time)
uv run nmwater export-site-data --out <dir>   # the bundle, including snow/
uv run python scripts/new_snow_validation.py  # regenerates new-snow-estimate.md
```
